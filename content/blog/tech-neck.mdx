Tech neck, also called text neck, is the informal name for the neck, shoulder and upper back ache that comes from long hours looking down at a phone, tablet or laptop. It is not a medical diagnosis, and the research behind it is a lot less dramatic than the posts warning that your phone is wrecking your spine. For most people the useful part is simple: move more often, bring the screen up, and build a little strength in the muscles that hold your head up.

## What is tech neck (text neck)?

Tech neck describes the stiffness and ache that builds up when your head spends hours tipped forward over a screen. The name became popular in the 2010s as smartphones took over, and "text neck" and "tech neck" are used for the same thing.

The usual signs are:

- an ache or tightness at the back of the neck and across the tops of the shoulders
- stiffness when you finally look up or turn your head after a long stretch on your phone
- soreness between the shoulder blades
- headaches that start at the base of the skull

It tends to come on gradually, over a long work week or a few heavy evenings on the sofa, and ease with movement. Pain that shoots into the arm, or comes with numbness or weakness, is a different picture, covered in the table below.

## Does looking at your phone cause tech neck? What the research says

Probably less than you have been told. A 2017 systematic review of 45 studies on smartphones and tablets found only limited evidence linking how much or how people use these devices to neck and shoulder symptoms. A 2018 study of 150 young adults found no link at all between the neck angle they used while texting and whether they had neck pain.

In adults the picture is a little different. A 2019 review found that adults with neck pain tend to hold their heads further forward than adults without it, and that a more forward head went along with more pain and disability. The same link did not show up in teenagers. Those studies took a single snapshot, though, so they cannot say whether the head position caused the pain or the pain changed the head position.

What this means in practice: the angle of your neck on its own is probably not the whole story. Hours of stillness, little movement between tasks, stress and weak neck and upper back muscles all seem to play a part. That is good news, because most of those can be changed without giving up your phone.

## Tech neck, pinched nerve or a headache from the neck?

Not every sore neck after a day on screens is tech neck. This is a rough guide to telling the common patterns apart.

| | Tech neck | [Pinched nerve in the neck](/conditions/pinched-nerve) | [Tension headache](/conditions/tension-headache) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where you feel it | Back of the neck, tops of the shoulders, between the shoulder blades | Neck, then into the shoulder blade, arm or hand on one side | A band of pressure around the head, often with tight neck and shoulders |
| Arm symptoms | Usually none | Typical: pain, tingling, numbness or weakness in one arm | Usually none |
| How it behaves | Builds with long screen time, eases when you move | Can be sharp or electric, and certain neck positions set it off | Comes and goes, often worse late in the day or under stress |
| What to do first | Change your setup and move more; book in if it is not easing after a week or two | Book an assessment, sooner if there is weakness or numbness | Book an assessment, and see a doctor first for any sudden or severe headache |

Real necks do not always fit one column. A stiff neck and a tension headache often turn up together, so the table tells you where to start reading rather than what you have.

## Setting up your phone, laptop and desk to ease tech neck

The goal is to bring the screen up towards your eyes, not your head down to the screen, and to stop holding any one position for too long.

<PostFigure src="/img/tech-neck-setup.webp" alt="Illustration of a man sitting upright at a desk with his laptop raised on a stand to eye level, a separate keyboard, and his phone held up near eye level" width={640} height={640} />

- **On your phone.** Lift it closer to eye level and drop your eyes, not your whole head. On the MRT or in a Grab, rest your elbow on your other arm or your bag so the phone comes up without your shoulder doing all the work.
- **On a laptop.** A laptop on its own forces a choice between a good screen height and a good keyboard height. If you use it for more than an hour a day, raise it on a stand or a pile of books and use a separate keyboard and mouse.
- **At a desk.** Top of the screen roughly at eye level, about an arm's length away, with your forearms supported and your feet flat.
- **On the sofa or in bed.** Prop the device on a pillow or your knees rather than holding it low in your lap for an hour.
- **Most of all, move.** Every 30 to 45 minutes, look up, roll your shoulders, turn your head both ways and walk somewhere, even just to the pantry. No setup is good enough to hold all day.

## 4 tech neck exercises for desk and phone users

These are gentle and need no equipment. Aim for a mild stretch or effort, never pain, and do them once or twice a day. Stop any that make your neck or arm worse.

### Chin tuck for tech neck

<PostFigure src="/img/tech-neck-chin-tuck.webp" alt="Illustration of a man sitting tall in side profile, with an arrow showing his chin drawing straight back" width={640} height={640} />

1. Sit or stand tall and look straight ahead.
2. Gently draw your chin straight back, as if making a double chin, without tipping your head down.
3. Hold for five seconds, then relax.
4. Repeat ten times.

### Shoulder blade squeeze

<PostFigure src="/img/tech-neck-shoulder-squeeze.webp" alt="Illustration of a man seen from behind on a stool, with arrows showing his shoulder blades drawing in towards the spine" width={640} height={640} />

1. Sit tall with your arms relaxed by your sides.
2. Draw your shoulder blades back and slightly down, as if tucking them into your back pockets.
3. Hold for five seconds without shrugging, then relax.
4. Repeat ten times.

### Doorway chest stretch

1. Stand in a doorway and rest your forearms on the frame, elbows at about shoulder height.
2. Step one foot forward and lean gently through the doorway until you feel a stretch across your chest.
3. Hold for 20 to 30 seconds.
4. Repeat three times.

### Upper back extension over a chair

<PostFigure src="/img/tech-neck-upper-back-extension.webp" alt="Illustration of a man seated with hands behind his head, arching his upper back backwards over the top of the chair, with a curved arrow showing the movement" width={640} height={640} />

1. Sit on a chair with a backrest that comes up to around your shoulder blades.
2. Support your head with your hands, elbows forward.
3. Lean back gently over the top of the backrest, letting your upper back arch, then return.
4. Repeat ten times, slowly. Skip this one if it hurts your lower back.

## When tech neck needs more than a better setup

Mild tech neck often eases within a week or two once your screen habits change. It is worth getting checked if it does not, if it keeps coming back, or if any of these apply:

- pain, tingling, numbness or weakness spreading into an arm or hand
- headaches that are getting more frequent or more severe
- neck pain after a fall, a knock or a road accident
- neck pain with fever, or with weight loss you cannot explain

## Tech neck: the bottom line

Tech neck is real discomfort with a dramatic name. The research does not support the scariest claims about phones and spines, but it does point to the same practical habits: raise the screen, move every half hour or so, and keep the muscles that hold your head up reasonably strong.
