Sciatica and piriformis syndrome both cause pain in the buttock and down the back of the leg, which is why they get mixed up. The difference is where the sciatic nerve is being irritated. Sciatica is the name for the symptom, nerve pain running down the leg, and its most common source is a disc or narrowing in the lower spine. Piriformis syndrome is one proposed cause that sits outside the spine, where the sciatic nerve passes under or through a small muscle deep in the buttock. It is far less common than a spinal cause, the diagnosis is still debated, and there is no single test that confirms it. This guide explains the difference, the clues that point one way or the other and how much each clue really tells you, and what a clinician checks.

## Sciatica vs piriformis syndrome: what is the difference?

Sciatica describes a symptom, while piriformis syndrome is one possible cause of it. That is the root of most of the confusion.

**Sciatica** is pain, tingling or numbness that follows the sciatic nerve from the buttock down the back of the leg, often past the knee and into the foot. The nerve is made up of nerve roots that leave the lower spine, so anything that irritates those roots can cause it. A slipped disc is the most common reason, followed by narrowing of the spinal canal, known as [spinal stenosis](/conditions/spinal-stenosis). Our [sciatica page](/conditions/sciatica) covers the full picture.

**Piriformis syndrome** is the idea that the sciatic nerve can be irritated further down its path, where it runs beneath the piriformis, a small muscle that runs from the base of the spine to the top of the thigh bone, deep in the buttock. If the muscle is tight, overworked or bruised, it may press on the nerve and cause sciatica-like pain without anything wrong in the spine.

## Is piriformis syndrome real? Why the diagnosis is still debated

Piriformis syndrome probably exists, but how often it happens is genuinely uncertain, and that is worth knowing before you settle on it as your answer. A systematic review of the condition noted that it has been described for over 70 years and remains controversial. In one survey it quoted, only 21 of 29 rehabilitation specialists in the United States believed the condition exists at all.

Part of the debate is about anatomy. In some people the sciatic nerve, or part of it, passes through the piriformis muscle instead of beneath it, and this was once thought to explain piriformis syndrome. A large review of cadaver and surgical studies found this variation in about 17% of people in general and about 16% of people operated on for piriformis syndrome. It was no more common in the people with symptoms, which suggests the anatomy alone is not the cause.

The practical upshot is simple. Because there is no agreed test, spinal causes are usually ruled out first. Piriformis syndrome is a reasonable explanation when the spine has been checked and the pattern fits, not a label to start with.

## How common is piriformis syndrome compared with disc-related sciatica?

Piriformis syndrome is much less common than sciatica from the spine. Estimates in the research vary widely, from under 1% to around 15% of the number of people with a disc herniation, depending on how strictly it was defined. A disc in the lower back remains the most common cause of sciatica.

That matters because the two are looked after differently. If a disc is the problem, stretching the buttock alone may miss it. If it is the piriformis, care aimed only at the spine may not help much.

## Sciatica or piriformis syndrome: the clues, and how much each one tells you

No single symptom separates the two. This table lists the clues people most often notice, what each tends to look like, and how much weight it carries on its own.

| Clue | Sciatica from the spine | Piriformis syndrome | How much it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where the pain starts | Lower back, then down the leg | Deep in one buttock | Some |
| Pain below the knee into the foot | Common | Less common | Some |
| Lower back pain at the same time | Often | Usually little or none | Some |
| Worse when sitting | Often | Often, especially on a hard seat or a wallet | Weak, since both are |
| Worse when you cough or sneeze | Common with a disc | Unusual | Some |
| Numbness, weakness or a reflex change in the leg | Common when a nerve root is compressed | Uncommon | Strong, points to the spine |
| Tender spot deep in the middle of the buttock | Sometimes | Usual | Some |
| Pain when the hip is turned in and the knee brought across the body | Sometimes | Often | Some |
| Straight leg raise reproduces the leg pain | Often | Less often | Some |

The clues overlap, and real people do not read the textbook. Weakness, numbness or a reflex change is the strongest single pointer, and it points towards the spine, not the buttock. ## How a clinician tells sciatica and piriformis syndrome apart

The assessment works through the spine first and the buttock second. A typical examination includes:

1. **History.** Where the pain started, what makes it worse, whether coughing or sneezing sends it down the leg, and any numbness, weakness or bladder changes.
2. **Lower back movement.** Bending and arching to see whether back movement reproduces the leg pain.
3. **Nerve tests.** Strength in the ankle and toes, reflexes at the knee and ankle, and skin sensation along the leg. Changes here point to a nerve root in the spine.
4. **Straight leg raise.** Lifting the straight leg while you lie on your back. Leg pain at a low angle suggests an irritated nerve root.
5. **Hip and buttock tests.** Turning the hip inward and drawing the knee across the body, known as the FAIR test, and pressing deep in the buttock over the sciatic notch. Pain here, with a clear spine, makes piriformis syndrome more likely.
6. **Imaging only when it would change the plan.** An MRI is not needed for most sciatica, but it is considered when there is weakness, symptoms are severe, or they are not settling after several weeks.

None of the hip tests is accurate enough on its own. It is the whole pattern, and a clear spine, that points towards the buttock. Our pages on [slipped disc](/conditions/slipped-disc) and [hip pain](/conditions/hip-pain) cover the other common lookalikes.

## Can you have sciatica and piriformis syndrome at the same time?

Yes, and it is more common than the either-or framing suggests. When a nerve root in the back is irritated, the muscles of the buttock often tighten and guard, so a sore piriformis can sit on top of a spinal problem. That is one reason buttock stretches sometimes help a little but do not settle things fully.

**Go to an emergency department straight away if you have:**

- loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness around the groin, buttocks or inner thighs
- weakness in both legs, or weakness that is quickly getting worse

**Book an assessment soon if:**

- a foot drags, or you cannot walk on your heels or toes
- numbness is spreading, or pain is so severe you cannot sleep
- the leg pain is not improving after two to three weeks
- the pain came on after a fall, or you have a history of cancer, fever or unexplained weight loss

## Sciatica vs piriformis syndrome: the bottom line

Sciatica is the symptom, and the spine is its most common source. Piriformis syndrome is one possible cause outside the spine, it is less common, and it is a debated diagnosis without a single test. Pain that starts in the back, runs past the knee, or comes with numbness or weakness points towards the spine. Pain centred deep in one buttock, worse with sitting and with a clear spine, makes piriformis syndrome more likely. Because they overlap, a proper assessment is the reliable way to tell them apart.
