A neck hump is a rounded bump where the back of your neck meets your upper back, and you may also hear it called a dowager's hump. Most neck humps come from the way the upper back has settled after years of leaning forward. Some come from changes in the bones, and a few are a pad of fat rather than anything to do with the spine. Which kind you have decides what is likely to help, so that is where this guide starts.

## What is a neck hump (dowager's hump)?

A neck hump is an exaggerated forward curve at the top of the upper back, usually around the bony knob at the base of your neck. Your upper back (the thoracic spine) is meant to curve gently forward. When that curve grows larger than usual, the medical name is hyperkyphosis, and the top of it shows as a hump, often with the head sitting forward of the shoulders.

"Dowager's hump" is the older name, from how often it was seen in older women. "Neck hump" is what most people search for after noticing it in a photo or the mirror. They usually mean the same thing.

It is common. Studies put an excessive upper back curve at roughly 20% to 40% of older adults, men and women alike, and it tends to increase with age. Younger people get a milder version too, usually from long hours bent over a phone or laptop.

## Posture, bone or fat: three kinds of neck hump

This table is a rough guide to the three kinds you are most likely to come across.

| | Posture hump | Fixed curve from bone changes | Fatty hump (buffalo hump) |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | The neck and upper back have settled into a forward, rounded position | The vertebrae have changed shape, for example wedged after a compression fracture, or with worn discs | A soft pad of fat over the base of the neck |
| How it feels | Firm, the shape of your spine | Firm and bony | Soft and squashy, sitting on top of the spine |
| When you stand tall | Usually gets smaller | Changes little | No change |
| Who tends to get it | Desk workers and heavy phone users, at any age | Older adults, especially women after menopause or anyone with low bone density | People with significant weight gain, long term steroid medicine, or a hormone condition such as Cushing's syndrome |
| What to do first | More movement, strength work and a better setup; book in if it bothers you | Get assessed, and ask your doctor about bone health | See a doctor first |

Many people have a bit of two columns at once, such as a posture hump sitting on top of some age related change in the bones. If screens are a big part of your day, our guide to [tech neck](/blog/tech-neck) covers setting up your phone, laptop and desk.

The fatty hump deserves a special word because it is the one we would send straight to a doctor. A soft hump that appears along with weight gain around the middle, a rounder face, purple stretch marks or easy bruising can be a sign of too much cortisol in the body. That needs blood tests, not exercises, and it is not something chiropractic or physiotherapy is for.

## Is your neck hump from posture? A simple wall check

A quick wall test can hint at how much of your neck hump is posture and how much is fixed.

<PostFigure src="/img/neck-hump-wall-check.webp" alt="Illustration of a woman standing in side profile against a wall, with her heels, bottom, shoulder blades and the back of her head touching it and her chin level" width={640} height={640} />

1. Stand with your heels close to a wall and your bottom and shoulder blades touching it.
2. Look straight ahead with your chin level, not tipped up.
3. Try to rest the back of your head on the wall.

If your head reaches the wall easily and the hump flattens, a good part of it is probably posture, which tends to respond to the exercises below. If you can only reach the wall by tipping your chin up, or there is a gap of several finger widths, the curve is stiffer and may include changes in the bones.

This is a home check, not a diagnosis. Clinicians use a measured version of the same idea, and an X-ray is the only way to see the bones themselves.

## Can a neck hump go away? What the research shows

Sometimes it gets smaller, but rarely as much as the videos online suggest. The honest answer depends on how much of the hump is posture and how much is bone.

The best evidence comes from older adults with a marked curve. In a randomised trial of 99 people aged 60 and over, six months of spine strengthening and posture training, three times a week with a physiotherapist, reduced the curve by about 3 degrees more than in a group that attended health talks instead. People in the exercise group also felt better about how they looked. Three degrees is real but small, and it took six months of steady work. An earlier review of 13 studies found most showed some improvement in posture with exercise, but the studies were too few and too varied to say by how much.

What this means in practice:

- A hump that is mostly posture often softens with regular strength work and fewer hours bent in one position.
- A curve from bone changes tends to change much less. The aim then shifts to keeping it from getting worse, staying strong, and easing the neck and shoulder ache that often comes with it.
- Any change takes months, not weeks. Be wary of anything that claims to flatten a neck hump in seven days.

## 5 neck hump exercises to try at home

These work the muscles that hold your head and upper back up, and stretch the chest muscles that tend to pull you forward. Do them once or twice a day, aim for gentle effort and never pain, and stop any that make your neck or arm worse. If you have osteoporosis or have had a spinal fracture, check with your doctor or clinician before starting, and skip the upper back extension.

### Chin tuck for a neck hump

<PostFigure src="/img/tech-neck-chin-tuck.webp" alt="Illustration of a man sitting tall in side profile, with an arrow showing his chin drawing straight back" width={640} height={640} />

1. Sit or stand tall and look straight ahead.
2. Gently draw your chin straight back, as if making a double chin, without tipping your head down.
3. Hold for five seconds, then relax.
4. Repeat ten times.

### Shoulder blade squeeze

<PostFigure src="/img/tech-neck-shoulder-squeeze.webp" alt="Illustration of a man seen from behind on a stool, with arrows showing his shoulder blades drawing in towards the spine" width={640} height={640} />

1. Sit tall with your arms relaxed by your sides.
2. Draw your shoulder blades back and slightly down, as if tucking them into your back pockets.
3. Hold for five seconds without shrugging, then relax.
4. Repeat ten times.

### Doorway chest stretch

<PostFigure src="/img/tech-neck-doorway-stretch.webp" alt="Illustration of a man standing in a doorway with his hands on the door frame, stepping forward, with an arrow pointing forward" width={640} height={640} />

1. Stand in a doorway and rest your hands or forearms on the frame, elbows at about shoulder height.
2. Step one foot forward and lean gently through the doorway until you feel a stretch across your chest.
3. Hold for 20 to 30 seconds.
4. Repeat three times.

### Upper back extension over a chair

<PostFigure src="/img/tech-neck-upper-back-extension.webp" alt="Illustration of a man seated with hands behind his head, arching his upper back backwards over the top of the chair, with a curved arrow showing the movement" width={640} height={640} />

1. Sit on a chair with a backrest that comes up to around your shoulder blades.
2. Support your head with your hands, elbows forward.
3. Lean back gently over the top of the backrest, letting your upper back arch, then return.
4. Repeat ten times, slowly. Skip this one if it hurts your lower back.

### Wall angels

<PostFigure src="/img/neck-hump-wall-angels.webp" alt="Illustration of a man standing with his back against a wall and both arms raised up the wall, with an arrow showing the arms sliding upward" width={640} height={640} />

1. Stand with your back against a wall, feet a small step away from it, and your bottom, upper back and head touching the wall if you can.
2. Raise your arms into a goal post shape, elbows bent at shoulder height, with the backs of your arms and hands against the wall as far as is comfortable.
3. Slowly slide your arms up the wall, keeping them in contact, then slide back down.
4. Repeat ten times. If your hands lift off the wall, only go as high as you can while keeping contact.

## When a neck hump needs checking

Get a neck hump looked at if it bothers you, keeps growing, or comes with any of these:

- it appeared or grew over weeks or months rather than years
- you have lost height, or your clothes have started to hang differently
- sudden sharp pain in the upper back, especially after a small knock, a cough or lifting something, which can be a sign of a compression fracture
- you are over 50 with a higher chance of low bone density, such as early menopause, long term steroid medicine or osteoporosis in the family
- the lump is soft and growing, or comes with weight gain, a rounder face or new stretch marks (see a doctor first)
- pain, tingling, numbness or weakness in an arm or hand

## Neck hump: the bottom line

A neck hump is common, usually harmless, and most often a mix of posture and age. The useful question is which kind you have. A posture hump tends to soften with months of strength work and fewer hours hunched in one position, a bony curve changes less but still benefits from staying strong, and a soft fatty hump belongs with your doctor.
