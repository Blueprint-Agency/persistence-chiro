Chiropractic for athletes and runners is mostly about keeping you training: finding out why something hurts, getting a stiff joint moving again, and building a plan so the same niggle does not end your season twice. What it cannot honestly promise is to make you faster or jump higher. The research on chiropractic and sport performance is thin, and we would rather say so up front than borrow a famous athlete's quote to suggest otherwise. This guide covers what the evidence actually shows, why runners and other athletes get injured, the common running injuries and what to do about each, what a sports assessment involves, and a few notes on training in Kuala Lumpur's heat.

## Does chiropractic improve athletic performance?

Not that anyone has shown convincingly. A review that searched the research on chiropractic and sport performance found only a handful of relevant studies, of mixed quality, and concluded there is not enough evidence to say chiropractic care directly improves performance. A few small studies have reported changes in things like hip range of motion after an adjustment, but small, short studies cannot tell you whether you will run a faster 10K.

Where chiropractic does fit for athletes is in the parts of sport that are about staying able to train:

- Working out why a joint or muscle hurts, and whether it is safe to keep training on it
- Restoring movement to stiff joints in the back, neck, hips or feet that may be changing how you move
- Spotting an injury early, before it becomes the kind that costs you weeks
- Working with physiotherapy on the strength and loading plan that gets you back to full training

Sports chiropractors were part of the athlete polyclinic in the Olympic Village at the Paris 2024 Games, alongside physiotherapists, osteopaths and sports massage therapists. That tells you the profession has a recognised place in sports medicine teams. It does not tell you an adjustment will win you a medal.

## Why runners and athletes get injured: training load

Most sports injuries that are not from a collision or a fall come down to load: doing more, faster, than the body has had time to adapt to. A widely cited review of training and injury in sport found that excessive and rapid increases in training load are likely responsible for a large share of non-contact soft tissue injuries. The same work found that athletes who build up a high training load gradually tend to be better protected, not worse.

Runners show the pattern clearly. A review of injury rates found that novice runners picked up running injuries at more than twice the rate of recreational runners per 1,000 hours of running, roughly 18 compared with 8. Previous injury and high weekly distance also stand out as risk factors in long distance runners.

In practice that means the riskiest times are when something changes quickly:

- Starting to run, or coming back after a break
- Jumping your weekly distance ahead of a race
- Adding hills, speed sessions or a new surface all at once
- Switching to very different shoes
- Squeezing extra badminton or futsal sessions in around a running plan

If a niggle shows up during one of these changes, the change is usually the first thing to look at.

## Common running injuries and what to do about each

Most running injuries settle with a change in load and some targeted strength work. This is a rough guide, not a diagnosis.

| Injury | What it usually feels like | Sensible first steps | Get it assessed if |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runner's knee (patellofemoral pain) | Ache around or behind the kneecap, worse on stairs, squatting or after sitting | Cut back hills and distance for a while, keep running pain free if you can, start hip and thigh strengthening | It has not eased after two or three weeks of lighter running, or the knee swells |
| IT band pain | Sharp pain on the outside of the knee that starts at a predictable point in a run | Shorten runs to below the point it starts, reduce downhill running, strengthen the hips | It stops you running at all, or keeps returning |
| Shin splints | Ache along the inner edge of the shin, often easing as you warm up | Reduce distance and impact, run on softer ground, build calf strength | Pain is in one small spot, hurts when you press on it, or aches at rest or at night. This can point to a stress fracture |
| Plantar heel pain | Sharp heel pain with the first steps in the morning or after sitting | Calf and foot strengthening, supportive shoes around the house, cut back for a while | It is getting worse or you are limping |
| Achilles pain | Stiffness and pain in the tendon above the heel, worst in the morning or at the start of a run | Reduce speed work and hills, start a gradual calf strengthening programme | The tendon is swollen, very painful to touch, or you felt a sudden snap |

Sudden pain with a pop, a joint that gives way, or swelling that appears quickly after an injury are reasons to get it checked promptly rather than run through it.

## What a sports assessment at our clinic involves

A sports assessment starts with how the injury happened and what your training looked like in the weeks before, because that is often where the answer is. Then we look at how you move, not only where it hurts. For a runner, that can mean watching a single leg squat, checking how the hips, knees and feet track, and testing strength on each side.

Your chiropractor examines the joints of the spine, pelvis and limbs for restricted movement, and our physiotherapists assess strength and control. At our Cheras clinic the two work together. Adjustments aim to restore movement where a joint is stiff, and the [sports injury rehabilitation](/services/sports-injury-rehabilitation) programme rebuilds strength in stages, with clear criteria for returning to full training. For tight, overworked muscles after a heavy block, [sports massage](/services/sports-massage) is another option.

If the findings suggest something that needs imaging or a doctor, such as a suspected stress fracture or a ligament tear, we refer you rather than adjust.

## Training in Kuala Lumpur's heat

Heat and humidity make every run harder, and they change what a sensible week looks like. A few things worth building in:

- **Run early or late.** Before 7am or after sunset avoids the worst of the heat on most days.
- **Expect to run slower.** The same effort costs more in 32 degrees and high humidity. Run by effort, not by your cool weather pace.
- **Drink before you are thirsty** on long runs, and replace salts on runs over an hour.
- **Build up gradually** if you are new to running in the heat, or returning after a holiday somewhere cooler.
- **Stop and get help** for dizziness, confusion, a headache that keeps building, or if you stop sweating. Heat illness is a medical emergency.

## The bottom line

Chiropractic will not turn you into a faster athlete, and anyone who promises that is selling something. What it can do is help you understand an injury, get stiff joints moving and, alongside physiotherapy, give you a plan to get back to training without carrying the same problem into the next block. Most running injuries trace back to doing too much too soon, so the most useful thing you can bring to an assessment is an honest account of your last few weeks of training.
