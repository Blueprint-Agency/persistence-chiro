Yoga can help a stiff, aching lower back, but some of the poses most often recommended for back pain are the ones a sore back finds hardest. Downward dog, pigeon and a deep standing forward bend all turn up on popular lists, and they suit some backs far better than others. This guide covers what the trials show about yoga for back pain, five gentle poses to start with, the poses to skip or change, which style of class to choose in KL (hot yoga included), and when to get your back checked first. It is written for the long drives, desk days and weekend chores that leave so many backs in Klang Valley sore by Friday.

## Does yoga help back pain? What the trials show

Yoga helps long standing low back pain about as much as other good exercise, which is a useful finding rather than a disappointing one. Two large trials compared it head to head with other options.

- **Yoga vs physiotherapy.** In a trial of 320 adults with long standing low back pain, 12 weekly yoga classes did as well as 15 physiotherapy visits for pain and for how much the back limited daily life. The gains held at one year in both groups, and fewer people in either group were still using pain medicine than in a group given only a self-help book. Minor aches after sessions were about as common with yoga as with physio.
- **Yoga vs stretching.** In a trial of 228 adults, 12 weekly yoga classes did better than a self-care book but no better than 12 weekly stretching classes. The benefits lasted several months.

Put together with the larger Cochrane review, which found a small benefit over no exercise and little difference from other exercise, the message is simple. Yoga is a reasonable way to keep a sore back moving, and the class you will actually keep going to matters more than whether it is called yoga. Our guide to [Pilates vs yoga](/blog/pilates-vs-yoga) goes through the Cochrane reviews in more detail.

## 5 yoga poses for lower back pain

These five are gentle, need little flexibility and avoid loading the back in a deep bend or twist. They are different from the moves in our [lower back pain exercises](/blog/lower-back-pain-exercises) guide, so you can use both. Move slowly, breathe through your nose, and stop any pose that causes sharp pain or sends pain down a leg.

### Supported child's pose

<PostFigure src="/img/yoga-back-child-pose.webp" alt="Illustration of a man kneeling on a yoga mat with his knees apart, sitting back towards his heels with his chest and head resting on a long cushion" width={640} height={640} />

1. Kneel on a mat with your knees apart and your big toes touching.
2. Place a firm cushion, bolster or two stacked pillows lengthwise in front of you.
3. Sit back towards your heels and lower your chest onto the cushion, turning your head to one side.
4. Let your lower back widen as you breathe out.
5. Stay for 1 to 2 minutes. If your knees complain, put a folded towel behind them.

### Sphinx pose

<PostFigure src="/img/yoga-back-sphinx.webp" alt="Illustration of a man lying face down on a yoga mat, propped on his forearms with his elbows under his shoulders and his upper body gently raised" width={640} height={640} />

1. Lie on your front with your legs straight and hip width apart.
2. Bring your elbows under your shoulders with your forearms flat on the mat.
3. Let your chest rise gently as you breathe in, without pushing up hard.
4. Keep your neck long and your buttocks relaxed.
5. Stay for 30 seconds to a minute. If it pinches in your lower back, slide your elbows further forward to lower the curve.

### Bridge pose

<PostFigure src="/img/yoga-back-bridge.webp" alt="Illustration of a man lying on his back on a yoga mat with his knees bent and feet flat, lifting his hips so his body forms a straight line from shoulders to knees" width={640} height={640} />

1. Lie on your back with your knees bent and feet flat, hip width apart.
2. Rest your arms by your sides.
3. Press through your heels and squeeze your buttocks to lift your hips until your body makes a straight line from shoulders to knees.
4. Hold for three slow breaths, then lower one section of your back at a time.
5. Repeat 8 to 10 times. Lift only as high as stays comfortable; this is not a backbend.

### Low lunge for tight hip flexors

<PostFigure src="/img/yoga-back-low-lunge.webp" alt="Illustration of a man in a low kneeling lunge on a yoga mat, front foot forward and back knee resting on a folded towel, sitting upright with his hands on his front thigh" width={640} height={640} />

1. Kneel on a folded towel and step one foot forward so the front knee is over the ankle.
2. Rest your hands on your front thigh and sit tall.
3. Gently tuck your tailbone under, then let your hips ease forward until you feel a stretch at the front of the back hip.
4. Keep your lower back from arching. The stretch should be in the hip, not the back.
5. Hold for 30 seconds on each side, twice. Long hours sitting in traffic or at a desk tend to leave these muscles tight.

### Lying hamstring stretch with a strap

<PostFigure src="/img/yoga-back-hamstring-strap.webp" alt="Illustration of a man lying on his back on a yoga mat with one knee bent and the other leg raised, holding a yoga strap looped around the sole of the raised foot" width={640} height={640} />

1. Lie on your back with both knees bent.
2. Loop a yoga strap, belt or towel around the sole of one foot.
3. Straighten that leg towards the ceiling, holding the strap with both hands, until you feel a stretch behind the thigh.
4. Keep your lower back resting on the mat and your shoulders relaxed. A slight bend in the knee is fine.
5. Hold for 30 seconds on each side, twice. This stretches the same muscles as a standing forward bend without the load on your back.

Two or three short sessions a week is a sensible start. Ten minutes on a weekday evening counts.

## Yoga poses to skip or modify with back pain

Some poses that appear on popular back pain lists are better left out or changed while your back is sore. A review of reported yoga injuries found that headstand, shoulder stand and lotus were among the postures most often involved, and it advised beginners to avoid them. That is a list of reported cases rather than a measure of risk, but it is a sensible place to start being careful.

| Pose | Why it can aggravate a sore back | Try instead |
|---|---|---|
| Downward dog | Tight hamstrings pull the lower back into a rounded position while it carries weight | Bend the knees well, or use supported child's pose |
| Pigeon | Loads the hip in a deep rotation and often twists the lower back with it | Lying hamstring stretch with a strap, or a gentle lying figure four |
| Standing forward bend with straight legs | A loaded forward bend of the lower back, hardest on a recent disc problem | Bend the knees and fold onto a chair, or the strap stretch lying down |
| Headstand, shoulder stand and plough | Heavy load through the neck and upper back, and easy to fall out of | Leave out entirely while you have back or neck pain |
| Lotus | Forces the knees and hips into a position many backs and knees resist | Sit cross legged on a cushion, or on a chair |
| Wheel and other deep backbends | Strong extension of the lower back joints | Bridge pose, kept low |
| Twisting while arching | Twisting and bending back at once compresses the small joints of the spine | Twist only from upright sitting, slowly and gently |

These poses are not off limits for good, and many people with healthy backs do them for years. The point is to wait until your back has settled and you have built some strength, and to do them under a teacher who knows about your back.

## Hot yoga, Hatha or chair yoga for back pain?

For back pain, a slow, gentle style is the safer place to start. Look for classes called Hatha, gentle, Yin, restorative, chair or therapeutic yoga, and keep them small enough that the teacher can watch you.

**Hot yoga** is popular in KL, and some studios promote it for back pain. The heat makes muscles feel looser, so many people stretch further than their back is ready for, and that is when a sore back tends to flare. Hot rooms also bring dehydration and dizziness, which matter if you take blood pressure medicine or are pregnant. If you enjoy hot yoga, wait until your back has settled, stay well within your range, and drink plenty of water before and after.

**Power and vinyasa** classes move fast from pose to pose, which leaves little time to adjust. They suit a back that is already strong rather than one that is still sore.

**Chair yoga** suits older adults, beginners and anyone who finds getting down to the floor hard. Our guide to [chair yoga](/blog/chair-yoga) explains how it works and who it suits.

Whatever class you choose, tell the teacher about your back before it starts, and skip anything that hurts. A good teacher will offer a change without making a fuss.

## When to get your back checked before yoga

Most people with an ordinary stiff or achy back can start gentle yoga straight away. Get your back assessed first if:

- pain, numbness or tingling spreads into your buttock or down your leg, which may be [sciatica](/conditions/sciatica)
- you have had a slipped disc in the last few months
- you have osteoporosis or have had a spinal fracture, since deep forward bends and strong twists may not be suitable
- the pain is new, getting worse, or wakes you at night
- the pain started after a fall or a road accident
- you notice weakness in a leg, or changes in bladder or bowel control, which needs urgent medical care

## Yoga for back pain: the bottom line

Yoga can help long standing back pain about as much as physiotherapy exercise or stretching, so the best choice is the one you will keep doing. Start with gentle poses like supported child's pose, sphinx, bridge, a low lunge and a strap hamstring stretch. Leave headstands, lotus, deep backbends and straight leg forward folds until your back has settled, and choose a slow, small class over a hot or fast one. If pain spreads down your leg or you have had a recent disc injury, get it checked first.
