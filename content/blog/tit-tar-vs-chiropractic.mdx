Tit tar and chiropractic both work on joints with the hands, and both can make that familiar crack, so it is easy to assume they are the same thing under two names. They are not. Tit tar (跌打) comes out of traditional Chinese medicine and the martial arts, while chiropractic is a separate profession with its own degree. In Malaysia the two are even registered as different practice areas under the same law. None of that makes one side good and the other bad. It does mean the questions worth asking before you lie down on either table are a little different.

## What is tit tar (跌打), and where does it come from?

Tit tar is the Cantonese reading of 跌打, *die da* in Mandarin, which literally means "fall hit". It is the branch of traditional Chinese medicine that grew up around knocks, falls, sprains and the injuries that came with martial arts training, which is why so many older tit tar masters also taught kung fu.

A tit tar session usually mixes several things: firm massage, pulling and manipulating joints, setting bones that are felt to be out of place, and often a herbal liniment rubbed in afterwards. That liniment is the *dit da jow* (跌打酒) many Malaysian households keep in a cupboard. Some practitioners add cupping, herbal plasters or acupuncture as well.

Training varies a lot. Some practitioners hold a university degree in traditional Chinese medicine, where bone setting is one module among many. Many others learned from a sifu or a family master over years of apprenticeship. Malay traditional bone setting, *urut tulang* or *urut patah*, is a separate tradition with a similar spirit, sitting under traditional Malay medicine instead.

## Tit tar vs chiropractic at a glance

Here is the short version, side by side. The details behind each row follow in the sections below.

| | Tit tar (跌打) | Chiropractic |
|---|---|---|
| Where it comes from | Chinese medicine and martial arts, centuries old | A profession founded in the United States in 1895 |
| Typical training | Varies widely, from a university Chinese medicine degree to apprenticeship under a sifu | A dedicated chiropractic degree |
| Regulation in Malaysia | Traditional Chinese medicine practice area, Act 775 | Chiropractic practice area, Act 775 |
| How the problem is worked out | Varies by practitioner, often by feel and movement, sometimes with pulse reading | Health history, physical and neurological checks, X-ray when the findings point to it |
| Hands-on work | Massage, joint manipulation and bone setting, often over a wide area | Specific adjustments to the joints found to be restricted |
| Extras | Herbal liniments, plasters, sometimes cupping | No herbs or ointments; exercise and posture advice |

The biggest practical difference is the middle row. How someone decides what to do shapes everything that follows, so that is the part worth paying attention to on a first visit, whichever door you walk through.

## That "crack" during tit tar or chiropractic: is a bone going back in?

Usually not. The sound most people hear during joint manipulation, from a sifu or a chiropractor, comes from a small gas cavity forming inside the joint as its two surfaces separate quickly. A 2015 study filmed this happening in real time on MRI, and the crack lined up with the moment the cavity appeared, not with a bone shifting back into a socket.

That study looked at finger joints, and the spine is more complicated, but the general point holds. A crack tells you a joint surface separated. It does not tell you that something was "put back", and it is not a measure of how good the session was. A louder or more frequent crack is not better, and a chiropractic adjustment that makes no sound at all is not a failed one.

This matters because "the bone went back in" is one of the most common ways both traditions get described, and it sets an expectation that one loud click should settle the problem. Real joint and muscle problems tend to be slower and more layered than that.

## How chiropractic assessment differs from a tit tar session

A chiropractor is trained to work out what is going on before deciding whether to adjust anything at all. At our clinic in Cheras that follows the six steps of the [Gonstead technique](/blog/gonstead-technique): a health history, a look at how your hips and shoulders sit, a nervoscope reading of skin temperature along the spine, hands-on checks of how each segment moves, an X-ray when the findings call for one, and only then an adjustment to the specific joints found to be stuck.

Tit tar assessment depends far more on the individual. Some masters spend a long time feeling along the spine and testing how you move. Others go fairly quickly to hands-on work across the whole sore area. Neither style is automatically wrong, but it is reasonable to ask any practitioner what they think is going on and why, before they start.

Part of an assessment is also deciding when hands-on work is the wrong call. If the history or examination suggests a fracture, an infection or a nerve problem that needs a doctor, the right move is to refer, not to adjust. ## Checking registration for tit tar and chiropractic in Malaysia

Both fall under the same law. Malaysia's Traditional and Complementary Medicine Act 2016 (Act 775) lists seven recognised practice areas, and traditional Chinese medicine and chiropractic are two separate ones. Since 1 March 2021, anyone practising in a recognised area has had to apply to register with the T&CM Council.

So the useful question is not just "are you registered?" but "registered in which practice area?". A practitioner registered in traditional Chinese medicine is not thereby registered as a chiropractor, and the reverse is true too. Ask for the registration number. A practitioner who has done the training and the paperwork will not mind being asked.

Very few people think to check. A survey of Klang Valley adults found that fewer than five in every hundred knew which Act governs chiropractic, even though almost half of those surveyed held a degree. Our guide to [what to check before booking a chiropractor in KL](/blog/best-chiropractor-kuala-lumpur) goes through the rest of that checklist.

## When to skip both tit tar and chiropractic and see a doctor first

Some problems should go to a doctor or the emergency department before anyone manipulates anything. That includes:

- a fall or knock where you cannot put weight on the limb, or the area is swollen, bruised or looks out of shape, which can mean a fracture
- a joint that looks dislocated
- numbness, pins and needles or weakness that is spreading or getting worse
- any change in bladder or bowel control alongside back pain
- back pain with a fever, or with weight loss you cannot explain
- pain after a road accident, even a slow one, where [whiplash](/conditions/whiplash) and other injuries need ruling out first
- a sudden, severe headache unlike any you have had before

Tit tar's whole tradition is built around falls and knocks, which is exactly where an undiagnosed fracture can hide. Firm manipulation over a broken bone, or over bones thinned by osteoporosis, can do real harm. An X-ray first costs very little by comparison. The same goes for chiropractic: if the history points this way, a responsible chiropractor will send you for imaging or to a doctor first, and so will we.
