A muscle knot is a small, tight patch of muscle that feels firm or tender when you press on it. The medical name is a myofascial trigger point. They are very common, most people get one at some point, and the usual causes are everyday things like sitting too long, stress, or overdoing an activity. Here is the honest headline: most muscle knots settle on their own with movement, gentle self care and a bit of time. Hands on treatment can help some people feel looser and more comfortable, though how much it helps, and how quickly, varies a lot from one person to the next. A few warning signs mean a knot is not simply a knot, and we cover those below so you know when to get it checked rather than rubbing at it.

## Key takeaways

**What is a muscle knot?** A muscle knot, or myofascial trigger point, is a tight band of muscle fibres that feels like a firm lump and can be tender or send a dull ache into a nearby area.

**Where do they show up most?** Most often across the upper back, shoulders and neck, the areas that hold your posture through a long day at a desk.

**Do muscle knots need treatment?** Usually not on their own. Many ease with movement, gentle stretching and time. Treatment is worth considering when a knot lingers, keeps coming back, or limits what you can do.

**Can a chiropractor get rid of a muscle knot?** We would rather be straight about this. Hands on care and dry needling may help a tight area feel looser and move more freely, but no single session reliably erases a knot, and results differ between people.

**When should I see someone?** If the pain is severe, follows an injury, or comes with numbness, weakness or fever, have it assessed rather than treated as a simple knot.

## What a muscle knot actually is

A muscle knot is a portion of muscle that stays partly contracted instead of fully relaxing. Press on it and you often feel a firm band, and sometimes that pressure reproduces a familiar ache. When the ache spreads to a nearby spot rather than staying under your finger, that is called referred pain, and it is one reason a knot in the shoulder can be felt as a headache or an ache down the arm.

The important thing to understand is that a knot is a soft tissue problem, not a bone out of place. Nothing has slipped or locked. The muscle is simply holding tension it has not let go of, usually because of how it has been loaded day after day.

## Why muscle knots form

Most knots trace back to load, not to anything dramatic. The common contributors are:

- **Sustained posture.** Hours in one position, especially the forward head and rounded shoulder posture of desk work, ask the same muscles to hold on without a break. Tissue tends to complain about staying still far more than about moving.
- **Sudden overuse.** A weekend of heavy lifting, a new workout, or a repetitive task the body is not used to can leave muscles tight and tender for days.
- **Stress.** People carry tension physically, often in the neck and upper shoulders, and clenched muscles fatigue faster.
- **Sleep, hydration and general load.** Poor sleep, an unsupportive pillow and being run down all make it harder for muscles to settle. None of these causes a knot on its own, but together they set the scene.

## What helps, and what to expect

For most knots, gentle movement and simple self care are the first and most useful steps, and often the only ones you need. Keeping the area moving within comfort tends to help more than resting it completely, which usually leaves things stiffer.

When a knot is stubborn or keeps returning, hands on options are worth a look. Manual therapy through our [physiotherapy](/services/physiotherapy) side uses soft tissue work and mobilisation to reduce the tension feeding the area. For trigger points that sit deep and are hard to reach by hand, [dry needling](/services/dry-needling) places a fine needle directly into the tight band, which some people find releases it more effectively than pressure from the outside. Both aim to make the area feel looser and move better. Neither works like an off switch, and your practitioner will tell you honestly what to expect in your case.

A note on what to expect afterwards: some soreness for a day is common after hands on work or needling, and it settles on its own. If a knot is really about how you sit and move all day, the lasting change comes from adjusting that, not from the treatment table alone.

## Massage, dry needling or adjustment: which is right for a knot?

People often ask which approach is best, and the honest answer is that it depends on the presentation, which is what an assessment is for. As a rough guide:

- **Massage and soft tissue work** are good for general tightness and for calming an area down. The effect is often pleasant and immediate, though for a persistent trigger point it may not last on its own.
- **Dry needling** targets a specific trigger point directly and tends to suit deep, stubborn knots that hands cannot easily reach. Read more about [dry needling](/services/dry-needling) and what a session involves.
- **Spinal adjustment** is aimed at restricted joints rather than at the knot itself. Where a stiff segment in the neck or upper back is part of why the surrounding muscle keeps loading up, improving how that joint moves can take some of the strain off. It is not a treatment for the knot in isolation.

In practice these are often combined, and which ones make sense is decided after looking at how you move, not before.

## Easing a knot at home

Most knots respond to a few simple, low risk habits:

- **Keep moving.** Gentle range of motion and a short walk stimulate blood flow far better than sitting still.
- **Stretch gently.** Ease into a stretch for the tight area and hold it comfortably. Never force it.
- **Use a warm compress** on a long standing tight area to help it relax, or a ball against a wall for gentle pressure on the spot.
- **Look at the trigger.** Raise your screen to eye level, alternate the shoulder your bag sits on, and stand and move every 30 to 45 minutes.
- **Support recovery.** Reasonable sleep, staying hydrated and not skipping rest after hard activity all give muscles a better chance to settle.

If a week or two of this changes nothing, that is a reasonable point to have the area looked at.

## When a knot needs more than self care

Most muscle knots are harmless. A few signs, though, mean the problem needs medical assessment rather than being treated as a simple knot. Get checked promptly if you notice:

- Numbness, tingling or weakness in an arm or hand
- Pain that follows a fall, car accident or direct blow
- A lump that is growing, hot, or comes with fever or feeling unwell
- Severe pain, or pain that steadily worsens rather than easing
- Night pain that wakes you, or unexplained weight loss

These are not typical of an ordinary muscle knot, and they point to something that should be assessed first. Persistent neck and shoulder tightness that does not settle is also worth a proper look, and our page on [neck pain](/conditions/neck-pain) covers that in more detail.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are muscle knots dangerous?** Muscle knots are almost always harmless, even when they are uncomfortable. The exceptions are the warning signs above, which are less about the knot and more about ruling out something else.

**How long does a muscle knot take to go away?** Many settle within a few days to a couple of weeks with movement and self care. One that lingers beyond that, or keeps returning to the same spot, is worth having assessed rather than simply waiting it out.

**Does cracking or massaging a knot fix it?** Rubbing a knot can ease it in the moment, and many people find that helpful. Whether it stays settled usually depends on changing what caused it, so relief on the table is a start rather than the whole answer.

**Is dry needling painful?** Most people describe a brief twitch or a dull ache rather than sharp pain, and some feel a little sore the next day. Tell your practitioner if anything feels worse than uncomfortable, because the technique can be adjusted.

**Can bad posture really cause muscle knots?** Sustained posture is one of the most common contributors, particularly the long hours of desk work. Movement breaks generally matter more than finding one perfect position, since the body tolerates most positions better than it tolerates holding any of them for hours.
